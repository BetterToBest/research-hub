Gold and silver since 1792

For most of US history the dollar was defined as a weight of metal. The Coinage Act of 1792 defined it in both silver and gold, at 15 ounces of silver to one of gold. The government's official price of gold barely moved for a century: $19.39 an ounce until 1834, then, after the Coinage Acts of 1834 and 1837, $20.67 until 1933. The chart shows the New York market price, which stayed close to the official one except in wartime. In 1933 private gold was called in, and in 1934 the price was raised to $35, where it stayed for official dealings until 1971. Since the link to gold ended, the price has been set in the market. The jump in the 1860s is the greenback period: from December 30, 1861 to the end of 1878 paper dollars could not be exchanged for gold, and for those years the chart shows gold's price in paper dollars.

The gold ban, 1933-1974. Executive Order 6102 (April 5, 1933) required everyone to deliver gold coin, gold bullion and gold certificates to a Federal Reserve bank by May 1, 1933, paid for in other money at face value, that is, at $20.67 an ounce. It exempted up to $100 of gold coin and certificates a person, coins of special value to collectors, and gold needed for industry, a profession or art. The Gold Reserve Act (January 30, 1934) moved the gold to the Treasury, and the next day Proclamation 2072 fixed the dollar at 15 5/21 grains of gold nine-tenths fine, which is $35.00 an ounce: gold handed in at $20.67 was revalued by 69 percent. Americans could not again own gold bullion freely until December 31, 1974, under Public Law 93-373.

Silver leaves the coins. Dimes, quarters and half dollars were 90 percent silver until the Coinage Act of 1965 (July 23, 1965) made dimes and quarters of copper-nickel and cut the half dollar to 40 percent silver. Silver certificates, paper money that could be exchanged for silver, stopped being exchangeable on June 24, 1968, under a 1967 law. On August 15, 1971 President Nixon announced that the dollar could no longer be converted into gold, ending the postwar Bretton Woods link; since then the dollar has not been tied to any metal.

Prices in other units

A price in dollars depends on what a dollar is worth. The same prices can be counted in ounces of gold, ounces of silver, or hours of work at the federal minimum wage. Each unit tells a different story, because gold, silver and the minimum wage have each moved on their own. None of them is the "true" price.

Two patterns stand out. Counted in hours at the federal minimum wage, a new house and a year of college cost far more than they did in the 1960s, because both rose faster than the minimum wage, which has not changed since 2009. Counted in gold, almost everything is cheaper than in the 1960s, because gold rose faster still. That is a statement about gold's price, not a sign that houses became easy to buy.

The minimum wage in silver

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 set the first federal minimum wage: 25 cents an hour from October 24, 1938, 30 cents a year later. It also set the longest week before overtime: 44 hours in the first year, 42 in the second, 40 after that. A week at the minimum wage can be measured in silver two ways:

  • In the coins it was paid in. Until 1964, a dollar in dimes, quarters or halves held 22.5 grams of pure silver, 0.7234 troy ounces (the coins weighed 2.5, 6.25 and 12.5 grams, 90 percent silver, under the Coinage Act of 1873). Worn coins hold a little less; dealers count 0.715 ounces a dollar. Since 1965 the coins hold none.
  • At the market price. How many ounces the week's pay would buy at that year's average price of silver.

For and against backing money with metal

Economists' main arguments, as Michael Bordo sets them out in his essay "Gold Standard" (Concise Encyclopedia of Economics, Econlib), with two essays from the Federal Reserve's history site on 1933 and 1971:

For

  • Prices steady over long periods. Under the classical gold standard, 1880-1914, US inflation averaged 0.1 percent a year, against 4.1 percent a year from 1946 to 2003.
  • A rule instead of discretion. The money supply is tied to the metal, so a government or central bank cannot simply create more; some see that as a discipline and an anchor for prices.
  • A promise that was kept. When the United States (1862-1879) and Britain left gold in wartime, both returned at the old price afterwards, which, Bordo writes, strengthened the rule's credibility.
  • Interest returns when inflation is high. Dissatisfaction with high inflation in the late 1970s and early 1980s renewed interest in the gold standard (Bordo).

Against

  • Prices unsteady from year to year. Bordo's measure of short-run price swings (the coefficient of variation) was 17.0 for 1879-1913 and 0.88 for 1946-1990.
  • Output and jobs less steady. The same measure for real output was 3.5 for 1879-1913 and 0.4 for 1946-2003; unemployment averaged 6.8 percent against 5.9 percent. A country on gold is less able to offset shocks, and shocks abroad move its money supply.
  • The cost of the metal. Real resources go into mining gold to hold as money; Milton Friedman put the cost for 1960 at more than 2.5 percent of national output.
  • Little room in a crisis. In early 1933, gold drained abroad and into private hands used up the Federal Reserve's free gold, and the New York Fed could no longer convert currency into gold. Scholars cited by the Federal Reserve's history site find that recovery from the Great Depression began when countries left gold and reflated, and that Roosevelt's reflation sped America's recovery (Richardson, Komai and Gou, "Roosevelt's Gold Program").
  • A fixed link can break. Under Bretton Woods, dollars held abroad came to exceed the gold the United States held at $35 an ounce, leaving it open to a run on gold; a run in London took gold to $40 on October 20, 1960 (Ghizoni, "Nixon Ends Convertibility of U.S. Dollars to Gold").

In January 2012 the University of Chicago's panel of leading US economists was asked whether replacing the Federal Reserve's discretionary policy with a gold standard, the dollar defined as a fixed weight of gold, would make price stability and employment better for the average American. None of the 36 who answered agreed: 15 disagreed and 21 strongly disagreed (Kent A. Clark Center, US Economic Experts Panel, "Gold Standard").

Bitcoin and other crypto

Bitcoin was set out in a nine-page paper, "Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System", under the name Satoshi Nakamoto: electronic cash sent directly between two people, with no bank or other trusted third party in between. The first block of its ledger is dated January 3, 2009. New coins are created by the network's own software, "since there is no central authority to issue them" (the paper). The price series used here, Coinbase's price as published by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, begins on December 1, 2014, at $370.

Bitcoin's yearly average price rose from $271 in 2015 to $101,581 in 2025, and its highest daily price in the series to the end of 2025 was $124,720 on October 5, 2025. Counted in gold, a bitcoin cost 0.23 ounces in 2015 and 29.5 ounces in 2025. Its swings were much larger than those of gold, shares or the dollar: from December 16, 2017 ($19,650) to December 15, 2018 ($3,183) it lost 84 percent of its price, and from November 8, 2021 ($67,510) to November 21, 2022 ($15,756) it lost 77 percent.

Early use on black markets. Silk Road was an anonymous online marketplace, reached only through the Tor anonymity software, that took payment only in bitcoin. A study by Nicolas Christin of Carnegie Mellon, measuring it over eight months in 2011-2012, found it was "overwhelmingly used as a market for controlled substances and narcotics", with sales of slightly over $1.2 million a month. The site ran for "nearly three years" (NPR) and closed after the arrest of its creator, Ross Ulbricht, in 2013. Prosecutors said it handled more than a million drug deals and more than $214 million in sales. Ulbricht was sentenced to life in prison on May 29, 2015, and was given a full pardon by President Trump on January 21, 2025 (NPR).

Collapses and fraud. FTX, then the world's third-largest crypto exchange, filed for bankruptcy in November 2022. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission charged that its customers' money had been mixed with, and used by, its affiliated trading firm, Alameda Research, causing the loss of over $8 billion in customer deposits. Its founder, Sam Bankman-Fried, was convicted of seven counts of fraud and conspiracy in November 2023 and sentenced to 25 years in prison on March 28, 2024. In August 2024 a federal court ordered FTX and Alameda to pay $12.7 billion to customers and other victims. The bankruptcy estate said in May 2024 that it owed about $11.2 billion and expected to have $14.5 billion to $16.3 billion to pay out; claims are counted at their November 2022 value, when bitcoin was about $16,080 (NPR). In 2024 the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center received 149,686 complaints involving crypto, reporting $9.3 billion in losses, more than half of the $16.6 billion lost to all the internet crime reported to it.

Meme coins and $TRUMP. A meme coin is a crypto token built around a joke, image or personality rather than a use. The official $TRUMP coin was launched on Friday, January 17, 2025, at nearly $6.50 and reached $73 two days later (NPR, from CoinGecko); CoinGecko's recorded high is $73.43 on January 19, 2025, and its price on November 18, 2026 was $0.97. Its issuer's site states that 1 billion coins are released over three years, 200 million at launch, and that "CIC Digital LLC, an affiliate of The Trump Organization, and Fight Fight Fight LLC collectively own 80%" of them, subject to a three-year unlocking schedule; 10 percent went to public distribution and 10 percent to liquidity. The site also says the coins "are not intended to be ... an investment opportunity". The President's financial disclosure for 2025 showed CIC Digital took in more than $600 million from sales of meme coins (PBS NewsHour).

Offshore money and corporate tax

The Panama Papers. On April 3, 2016, more than 100 news organisations coordinated by the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists (ICIJ) began publishing stories from 11.5 million documents of Mossack Fonseca, a Panamanian law firm that set up offshore companies. The documents had been obtained by the German newspaper Süddeutsche Zeitung. They showed heads of state, officials, criminals and celebrities holding assets through secret companies in tax havens. Owning an offshore company is not in itself illegal; the papers showed it being used, in many cases, to hide ownership.

  • What followed. Within 48 hours Iceland's prime minister, Sigmundur Davíð Gunnlaugsson, announced he would step down; a Spanish minister also resigned; Pakistan's prime minister was later sent to prison for corruption; New Zealand changed its laws. By ICIJ's 2025 tally, governments have recovered more than $1.3 billion in back taxes and penalties as a direct result, a figure ICIJ says almost certainly understates the effect because many governments did not report. Mossack Fonseca closed within two years. In April 2026 a German court convicted a former co-owner of aiding and abetting tax evasion.
  • What did not. In June 2024 a Panamanian court acquitted all 28 people tried there over shell companies used in bribery scandals, including co-founder Jürgen Mossack; both founders had been acquitted in a separate money-laundering case in 2022. Many governments never reported what they recovered.
  • Daphne Caruana Galizia. The Maltese journalist was reporting on the Panama Papers' revelations in Malta when she was killed by a car bomb outside her home in Bidnija on October 16, 2017. In 2021 a public inquiry of former judges found that the Maltese state bore responsibility, by failing to act on threats to her life and creating an "atmosphere of impunity". Two brothers were sentenced to 40 years in October 2022 after pleading guilty; an accomplice is serving 15 years; a businessman charged with ordering the killing denied it and was awaiting trial (ICIJ, October 2022).

Profits shifted offshore. Thomas Tørsløv, Ludvig Wier and Gabriel Zucman estimate that close to 40 percent of multinational companies' profits are shifted to tax havens each year (close to $1 trillion in 2019), cutting corporate tax revenue worldwide by about 10 percent, and that US multinationals shift about 60 percent of their foreign profits. The EU Tax Observatory's Global Tax Evasion Report 2024 puts the amount at $1 trillion in 2022, 35 percent of the profits multinationals book outside their home country. These are estimates built from national accounts and affiliates' statistics, not totals from tax records.

The corporate income tax brought in 27.6 percent of federal receipts on average in the 1950s and 8.2 percent from 2018 to 2025 (8.6 percent in 2025). Measured against corporate profits it fell from about 40 percent in the 1950s to about 11 percent from 2018 to 2025. The legal rate was cut from 35 to 21 percent from 2018.

Profitable companies that paid no federal income tax. The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy (ITEP), a nonprofit that studies tax policy, reads companies' annual reports. Of 342 large companies that were profitable in each year from 2018 to 2022, it found that 23 paid no federal income tax over the five years and 109 paid none in at least one year; the 342 paid an average of 14.1 percent of their profits, against the 21 percent legal rate (February 2024). For 2025, it found that 114 of 706 profitable large companies paid no current federal income tax, and that the 706 together paid 10.4 percent, the lowest rate it has found in about 40 years of these studies (October 2026). ITEP's figures use the tax the companies report to shareholders, which can differ from the tax shown on their returns, which are not public.

Against the rate for small firms. Since 2018 the legal rate is a flat 21 percent for every taxable corporation, whatever its size; ITEP attributes the lower rates it finds to tax breaks such as accelerated depreciation, credits and losses carried between years. No opened source gave the average rate that small firms actually pay, so none is shown.

College costs and student debt

A year of undergraduate tuition, fees, room and board, averaged across all colleges, cost $13,122 in 1963-64 in 2025 dollars, and $30,247 in 2023-24: 2.3 times as much after inflation. At the federal minimum wage in force on July 1 of the year the school year began (the rule used throughout this page; $1.15 in 1963, before the rise to $1.25 that September), it took 1,085 hours of work in 1963 and 3,949 hours in 2023. The chart in Prices in other units shows every year (choose "A year of college").

Federal insurance of student loans began with the Higher Education Act of 1965 (November 8, 1965), Part B, "Federal, State, and private programs of low-interest insured loans to students". Student loans owed by US households were $0.24 trillion at the start of 2003 and $1.66 trillion in the third quarter of 2026 (Federal Reserve Bank of New York), 8.7 percent of all household debt.

Student loans in bankruptcy. Most debts can be wiped out in bankruptcy. Congress has narrowed that for student loans step by step:

  • 1976. The Education Amendments of 1976 (October 12, 1976) allowed a federally guaranteed student loan to be discharged in bankruptcy only after five years of repayment, or earlier if repayment would impose an "undue hardship" on the debtor or the debtor's dependents (from September 30, 1977).
  • 1978. The Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978 moved the rule into the new Bankruptcy Code, section 523(a)(8): five years, or undue hardship.
  • 1990. The Crime Control Act of 1990 lengthened the wait to seven years.
  • 1998. The Higher Education Amendments of 1998 removed the waiting-period route: from then on a federal student loan can be discharged only on undue hardship.
  • 2005. The Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 extended the same rule to private student loans that qualify for the tax deduction.

The law does not define "undue hardship". In Brunner v. New York State Higher Education Services Corp. (decided October 14, 1987) the federal appeals court for the Second Circuit adopted a three-part test: the debtor cannot keep a "minimal" standard of living for herself and her dependents if made to repay; this is likely to last for a significant part of the repayment period; and she has made good-faith efforts to repay.

Against corporate bankruptcy. The student-loan exception applies only to debtors who are individuals. When a court confirms a company's Chapter 11 plan, the confirmation discharges the company from the debts that arose before it, as the plan provides (11 U.S.C. 1141(d)). In a leveraged buyout, a private equity firm buys a company mostly with money borrowed against the company itself. Brian Ayash and Mahdi Rastad (Finance Research Letters, 2021) followed 467 public companies taken private in leveraged buyouts from 1980 to 2006, with a matched group of similar companies that stayed public: about 20 percent of the buyout companies went bankrupt within ten years, against about 2 percent of the matched companies (as reported by Institutional Investor, July 2019). The study compares outcomes; it does not show that every bankruptcy was caused by the buyout.

Who owns the news

The Telecommunications Act of 1996 (February 8, 1996) told the Federal Communications Commission to remove its limit on the number of radio stations one company could own nationally, to raise the local limits (up to 8 stations in the largest markets), and to remove the limit on the number of television stations one company could own nationally, keeping a cap of 35 percent of the national audience.

Counts of "how many companies own the media" depend on what is counted: newspapers, local TV, radio, cable channels, or all of them, and by stations, audience or revenue. The counts below each say what they cover.

  • Local television. In 2004 the five largest local TV companies (Sinclair, Nexstar, Gray, Tegna and Tribune) owned, operated or serviced 179 full-power stations; by 2016 they had 443, an estimated 37 percent of all full-power local stations (Pew Research Center, from company filings and BIA Kelsey data). In 2014 Pew counted five companies owning one-third of the about 1,400 local TV stations.
  • Local newspapers. The Medill School at Northwestern University counted 213 US counties with no local news source in 2025, up from about 150 twenty years earlier, and 1,524 more with only one; 136 newspapers closed in the year to its 2025 report, and newspapers have lost more than three-quarters of their jobs since 2005.
  • All media. A widely repeated count, that about 50 companies controlled most US media in 1983 and five or six do today, traces to the journalist Ben Bagdikian's books. The books were not opened for this page, so that count is not used here.

What this does not show

  • Yearly averages. Every price is a yearly average. Within a year the price of silver or gold can move by half or more (silver in 1980, for example), so a single month can look very different.
  • The coin measure is a way of counting, not how people were paid. In 1938 most wages were paid by cheque or in paper money, not in silver coin. The coin measure says what the silver in that many dollars of coin weighed.
  • Who was covered. The 1938 Act covered workers in interstate commerce and goods for it, a minority of workers at first; coverage widened with later amendments, and many states now set higher minimums. The series is the federal rate, not what low-paid workers earned.
  • Quality changes. A new house, a year of college or a basket of medical care in 2025 is not the same thing as in 1963. The price indexes adjust for some of this; the house and college prices do not.
  • Correlation is not cause. This page sets laws and prices side by side; it does not test what caused what.
  • Crypto prices are one exchange's. The bitcoin series is Coinbase's daily price; other exchanges differ a little, and earlier prices (2010-2014) are not in an official series.
  • Estimates, not tallies. The profit-shifting figures are researchers' estimates from national accounts; ITEP's rates use the tax companies report to shareholders, not their tax returns, which are not public; the Panama Papers recovery tally covers only what governments reported to ICIJ.
  • Reports of court cases are dated. Court outcomes are given as of the report cited; later appeals or trials can change them.
